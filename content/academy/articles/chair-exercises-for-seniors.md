A sturdy chair is one of the best pieces of exercise equipment you can own. It gives you support when your balance is shaky, takes pressure off sore knees and hips, and lets you work your legs, arms, core, and posture without getting down on the floor.

This guide gives you 12 chair exercises for seniors, grouped into warm-up, strength, and balance and mobility, with sets, reps, form cues, and easier and harder options. At the end there is a simple weekly plan you can follow for a month.

If you are over 65, have heart or lung problems, recent surgery, joint replacements, or a history of falls, check with your doctor or physical therapist before starting. Stop any move that causes sharp pain, dizziness, or chest discomfort.

## How much exercise older adults need

The CDC recommends that adults 65 and older aim for 150 minutes a week of moderate activity (like brisk walking), at least 2 days a week of muscle-strengthening activity, and activities that improve balance. Chair exercises cover the strength and balance parts well, and a brisk seated routine can raise your heart rate too.

Ten minutes of chair work most days is a good start, and you can add a few minutes each week.

## Set up your chair first

- Use a solid chair with no wheels and no arms if possible. A kitchen or dining chair works well.
- Put the back of the chair against a wall so it cannot slide.
- Sit toward the front half of the seat, feet flat, knees bent about 90 degrees, hips a little higher than or level with your knees.
- Sit tall, as if a string is pulling the top of your head toward the ceiling.
- Keep water nearby and wear shoes with good grip.

## Warm-up (3 to 5 minutes)

### 1. Seated marching
Lift one knee a few inches, lower it, then the other, like marching in place. Pump your arms if that feels good.
- **Do:** 1 to 2 minutes.
- **Easier:** Lift your heels only, keeping toes down.
- **Harder:** March faster or lift knees higher.

### 2. Shoulder rolls
Roll both shoulders up, back, and down in slow circles. Then reverse.
- **Do:** 10 each direction.

### 3. Ankle circles and toe taps
Lift one foot slightly and draw slow circles with your toes, then tap your toes up and down. Switch feet.
- **Do:** 10 circles each way, 10 taps, each foot.

## Strength moves (10 to 15 minutes)

Do each move for the reps listed, rest 30 to 60 seconds, and repeat for 1 to 3 rounds. Move slowly. Breathe out on the effort.

### 4. Sit-to-stand (chair stand)
This is the most useful move on the list because it trains the exact strength you use to get out of bed, off the toilet, and out of the car. Scoot to the front of the chair, feet hip-width apart and slightly behind your knees. Lean your chest forward a little, press through your heels, and stand up. Sit back down slowly, with control.
- **Do:** 8 to 12 reps.
- **Easier:** Push off with your hands on your thighs or the chair arms, or put a firm cushion on the seat to make it higher.
- **Harder:** Cross your arms over your chest, or take 3 seconds to lower down.

### 5. Seated knee extensions
Sit tall and straighten one leg until it is level with the seat, squeeze the front of your thigh for 2 seconds, then lower it.
- **Do:** 10 to 12 reps per leg.
- **Easier:** Straighten the leg only partway.
- **Harder:** Add a light ankle weight.

### 6. Seated leg lifts (hip flexors)
Keep your knee bent and lift your whole thigh off the seat a couple of inches. Hold for 2 seconds and lower.
- **Do:** 8 to 10 per leg.

### 7. Seated heel raises
With feet flat, press through the balls of your feet and lift your heels as high as you can. Lower slowly. Stronger calves help with walking and balance.
- **Do:** 15 to 20 reps.
- **Harder:** Press down on your knees with your hands for resistance, or do them standing behind the chair.

### 8. Seated overhead press
Hold light dumbbells, water bottles, or soup cans at shoulder height, palms forward. Press up until your arms are nearly straight, then lower. If your shoulders bother you, press forward at chest height instead.
- **Do:** 8 to 12 reps.

### 9. Seated row
Hold a resistance band around your feet, or with no band, reach your arms forward. Pull your elbows back past your ribs and squeeze your shoulder blades together. This helps posture and upper back strength.
- **Do:** 10 to 12 reps.

### 10. Seated core brace
Sit tall, hands on your thighs. Pull your belly button gently toward your spine and hold while breathing normally. Then try lifting one foot an inch off the floor while you stay tall.
- **Do:** Hold 10 to 20 seconds, 3 to 5 times.
- **Harder:** Slowly twist your shoulders left and right while holding the brace.

## Balance and mobility (5 minutes)

### 11. Standing balance hold with chair support
Stand behind the chair with both hands on the backrest. Shift your weight to one foot and lift the other slightly. Hold, then switch. As you improve, hold with one hand, then a fingertip.
- **Do:** 10 to 30 seconds per leg, 2 to 3 times.
- Only do this one standing with the chair in front of you and a wall or counter nearby.

### 12. Seated hamstring stretch and side reach
Straighten one leg out with the heel on the floor, toes up. Sit tall and hinge forward from your hips until you feel a gentle stretch behind the thigh. Hold, switch legs. Then reach one arm overhead and lean gently to the opposite side.
- **Do:** Hold each stretch 20 to 30 seconds, breathing slowly.

## A simple weekly plan

| Day | What to do | Time |
|---|---|---|
| Monday | Warm-up + strength moves 4 to 10, 1 to 2 rounds + stretch | 20 min |
| Tuesday | Warm-up + seated marching intervals (1 minute fast, 1 minute easy, 5 times) + balance | 15 min |
| Wednesday | Rest or an easy walk | 10 to 20 min |
| Thursday | Warm-up + strength moves 4 to 10, 1 to 2 rounds + stretch | 20 min |
| Friday | Warm-up + balance holds + stretches | 10 min |
| Saturday | Walk or seated marching to music | 15 to 20 min |
| Sunday | Rest | - |

After 2 to 3 weeks, if the moves feel easy, add a round, add a few reps, or use a slightly heavier weight. Write down your sit-to-stand reps each week. Watching that number climb from 6 to 10 to 12 tells you your legs are getting stronger. A [free workout tracker](/app/) makes this easy and shows you your streak.

## Safety tips

- Never hold your breath. Breathe out as you push or lift.
- Move slowly. Control matters more than speed.
- Mild muscle tiredness is normal. Sharp pain, joint pain that lingers, dizziness, or shortness of breath means stop and rest.
- If you have arthritis, warm up longer and keep movements in a comfortable range. If your knees complain, our guide to [exercises for bad knees](academy:exercises-for-bad-knees) has more gentle options.

## Frequently asked questions

### How often should seniors do chair exercises?
Aim for strength moves 2 to 3 days a week with a day of rest between, which lines up with the CDC's guidance of at least 2 days of muscle strengthening for older adults. Gentle marching, balance, and stretching can be done most days. Start with 10 minutes and build up.

### Can chair exercises help seniors lose weight or belly fat?
Chair exercises burn calories and build muscle, which can support weight loss alongside eating habits and daily walking. No exercise burns fat from one specific spot, so seated ab moves will not target belly fat alone. Talk with your doctor before starting a weight loss plan.

### Are chair exercises good for seniors over 80?
Yes, many people in their 80s and 90s use chair exercises safely. Start with the easier version of each move, fewer reps, and a longer rest, and get your doctor's OK first, especially if you have heart problems or have fallen recently.

### What are the best chair exercises for balance?
Sit-to-stands, heel raises, and supported standing balance holds behind a chair all help. Stronger legs and ankles make it easier to catch yourself if you trip. Always keep the chair and a wall or counter within reach when you stand.

### Do chair exercises help with lower back pain?
Gentle seated core bracing, side reaches, and hamstring stretches feel good for many people with a stiff lower back. If your back pain is severe, new, or spreads down your leg, see your doctor or a physical therapist before exercising.
