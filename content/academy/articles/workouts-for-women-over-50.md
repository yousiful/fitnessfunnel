After 50, a lot of women notice that the workouts that used to work stop working. Long cardio sessions leave you tired but not stronger. Joints complain more. Weight shifts to new places. Your body is changing, and the way you train can change with it.

The good news is that the most useful workouts for women over 50 are simple, short, and can be done at home with a pair of dumbbells or a resistance band. Below is what to focus on, the exact moves, and a sample week.

## What the guidelines say

The CDC recommends that adults get at least **150 minutes of moderate-intensity activity a week**, such as brisk walking, plus **muscle-strengthening activities on two or more days a week** that work all the major muscle groups. For adults 65 and older, the CDC also recommends activities that improve balance.

That is the full target. You do not need to hit it in week one. Starting with two short strength sessions and a few walks is a great place to begin.

## Why strength training matters most after 50

Estrogen drops around menopause, and that affects both muscle and bone. The Bone Health and Osteoporosis Foundation notes that women can lose up to 20 percent of their bone density in the five to seven years after menopause. Muscle also declines with age if we do not use it. Harvard Health Publishing reports that inactive adults lose roughly 3 to 5 percent of their muscle mass per decade after age 30.

Strength training puts load on muscles and bones, and both respond by getting stronger. It also makes daily life easier: carrying groceries, getting up off the floor, lifting a grandchild, climbing stairs without holding the rail.

If you have osteoporosis or osteopenia, ask your doctor or a physical therapist which moves are right for you before you start. Some exercises, like deep forward bends with weight, may need to be avoided.

## The four parts of a good week

1. **Strength** two or three days a week
2. **Low-impact cardio** most days (walking counts)
3. **Balance** a few minutes, several days a week
4. **Mobility** a few minutes after each workout

## The strength workout

Do this two or three times a week, with at least one rest day between sessions. Rest 60 to 90 seconds between sets.

**Equipment:** a pair of light dumbbells (5 to 10 lb is a common start), a resistance band, and a sturdy chair.

### 1. Sit-to-stand (or goblet squat)
2 to 3 sets of 8 to 12 reps.
Sit on the front edge of a chair, feet hip-width apart. Lean slightly forward and stand up by pushing through your whole foot. Sit back down slowly, taking about three seconds.
**Easier:** use your hands on your thighs to help. **Harder:** hold a dumbbell at your chest and squat to just above the chair without sitting.

### 2. Wall or counter push-up
2 to 3 sets of 8 to 12 reps.
Hands on a wall or kitchen counter, a bit wider than your shoulders. Keep your body in a straight line from head to heels and lower your chest toward your hands.
**Easier:** stand closer to the wall. **Harder:** move to a lower surface like a sturdy couch arm, then the floor on your knees.

### 3. Bent-over dumbbell row
2 to 3 sets of 10 to 12 reps.
Place one hand on a chair seat, step back, and hinge forward with a flat back. Pull the dumbbell toward your hip, squeezing your shoulder blade back. Lower slowly.
**Easier:** use a resistance band anchored in a door. **Harder:** go heavier, or pause for one second at the top.

### 4. Glute bridge
2 to 3 sets of 10 to 15 reps.
Lie on your back, knees bent, feet flat. Press through your heels and lift your hips until your body forms a line from shoulders to knees. Hold one second, then lower.
**Easier:** smaller range. **Harder:** place a dumbbell across your hips, or try one leg at a time.

### 5. Step-up
2 sets of 8 to 10 reps per leg.
Use a bottom stair. Step up with one foot, press through that heel to stand tall, then step down slowly. Hold the rail if you need it.
**Easier:** a lower step. **Harder:** hold dumbbells at your sides.

### 6. Overhead press
2 sets of 8 to 12 reps.
Sit or stand tall with dumbbells at shoulder height. Press them up without arching your lower back, then lower slowly.
**Easier:** lighter weights or one arm at a time. **Harder:** standing, with slightly heavier weights.

### 7. Dead bug
2 sets of 6 to 8 reps per side.
Lie on your back, arms up, knees bent over hips. Slowly lower your opposite arm and leg toward the floor while keeping your lower back gently pressed down. Return and switch.
**Easier:** move only the legs. **Harder:** slow it down to a four-second count each way.

**How heavy?** Pick a weight where the last two reps of each set feel hard but your form stays clean. When you can do the top of the rep range easily for two workouts in a row, add a little weight or a few reps.

## Balance work (5 minutes)

Do these near a counter so you can hold on if needed.

- **Single-leg stand:** 3 holds of 20 to 30 seconds per leg. Progress by letting go of the counter, then by turning your head slowly side to side.
- **Heel-to-toe walk:** 2 passes of 10 to 15 steps along a hallway.
- **Side leg raise:** 10 slow lifts per side while standing tall.

## Low-impact cardio

Brisk walking is the easiest place to start. Aim for a pace where you can talk but not sing. Swimming, cycling, and dancing also count. If your knees bother you, our guide to [exercises for bad knees](academy:exercises-for-bad-knees) has joint-friendly options that still get your heart rate up.

## A sample week

| Day | Workout |
|---|---|
| Monday | Strength workout + 5 min balance |
| Tuesday | 30 min brisk walk |
| Wednesday | Strength workout + 5 min balance |
| Thursday | 30 min walk or bike + gentle stretching |
| Friday | Strength workout (or rest if you are new) |
| Saturday | 30 to 45 min walk, swim, or dance class |
| Sunday | Rest or an easy stroll |

New to exercise? Start with two strength days and three 15-minute walks, then build up over a month or two.

## Staying safe

Check with your doctor before starting a new program, especially if you have heart conditions, high blood pressure, joint pain, osteoporosis, or have not exercised in a long time. Muscle soreness a day or two after a workout is normal. Sharp pain, dizziness, or chest discomfort is not, so stop and get it checked.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the best workout for women over 50?
A mix of strength training two or three days a week, low-impact cardio like brisk walking, and a few minutes of balance work. Strength training is the piece most often missing, and it supports muscle, bone, and everyday movement.

### Can women over 50 build muscle?
Yes. Muscle responds to strength training at any age. Use a weight that feels hard for the last couple of reps, increase it slowly over time, and eat enough protein to support recovery.

### How often should a woman over 50 work out?
The CDC recommends 150 minutes of moderate activity a week plus muscle-strengthening on at least two days. A simple way to get there is 30 minutes of walking five days a week and two or three short strength sessions.

### Should women over 50 lift weights?
For most women, yes. Lifting weights is one of the best ways to keep muscle and support bone health after menopause. Start light, focus on form, and check with your doctor first if you have osteoporosis or a joint condition.

### What exercises help women over 50 lose belly fat?
No exercise burns fat from one spot. Strength training, regular walking, good sleep, and a steady eating plan together help reduce overall body fat, including around the middle. Check with your doctor before starting a weight loss plan.
