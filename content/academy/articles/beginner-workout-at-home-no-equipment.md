You don't need a gym, dumbbells, or a fancy mat to get stronger. Your body weight, a sturdy chair, a wall, and a bit of floor space are enough for the first several months of training. This plan gives you eight moves, a 20-minute routine you can do three times a week, and clear ways to make each move easier or harder as you go.

If you have joint pain, a heart condition, are pregnant or recently gave birth, or haven't been active in a long time, check with your doctor before you start. During any workout, stop if something hurts sharply. Muscle effort and mild burning are normal. Sharp, pinching, or joint pain is a signal to back off.

## What you need

- About 2 meters (6 feet) of clear floor space
- A sturdy chair that won't slide (put it against a wall)
- A wall
- A towel or carpet for floor moves
- Water and a timer on your phone

Shoes are optional. If you work out on a hard floor, supportive sneakers help with the standing moves.

## How much exercise beginners actually need

The CDC recommends adults get 150 minutes of moderate activity a week, plus muscle-strengthening activity on 2 or more days. That sounds like a lot when you're starting from zero, so don't aim for it in week one. Three 20-minute sessions of this routine give you 60 minutes and cover your two strength days. Add a few short walks and you're well on your way.

## The 8 moves

Do each move with slow, controlled reps. Quality beats speed every time.

### 1. Chair squat
Stand in front of a chair, feet about hip-width apart. Push your hips back and lower until you lightly touch the seat, then stand up by pressing through your whole foot.
- **Form cue:** Keep your chest proud and your knees pointing the same direction as your toes.
- **Easier:** Sit fully on the chair between reps and use your hands on your thighs to help you up.
- **Harder:** Hover an inch above the chair without sitting, or pause for 2 seconds at the bottom.

### 2. Wall push-up
Stand an arm's length from a wall, hands on the wall at chest height, slightly wider than your shoulders. Bend your elbows to bring your chest toward the wall, then push back.
- **Form cue:** Body in one straight line from head to heels. Don't let your hips sag or stick out.
- **Easier:** Stand closer to the wall.
- **Harder:** Move to a kitchen counter, then to your knees on the floor.

### 3. Glute bridge
Lie on your back, knees bent, feet flat and hip-width apart. Squeeze your glutes and lift your hips until your body makes a line from shoulders to knees. Lower slowly.
- **Form cue:** Push through your heels and avoid arching your lower back at the top.
- **Easier:** Lift halfway.
- **Harder:** Hold the top for 3 seconds, or march one foot off the floor at a time.

### 4. Standing march
March in place, lifting each knee toward hip height and swinging your arms. This warms you up and gets your heart rate going without jumping.
- **Easier:** Smaller knee lifts, one hand on the wall for balance.
- **Harder:** March faster or add a gentle jog in place.

### 5. Bird dog
Start on hands and knees, hands under shoulders and knees under hips. Reach your right arm forward and your left leg back, hold for 2 seconds, then switch.
- **Form cue:** Imagine a glass of water on your lower back. Don't spill it.
- **Easier:** Move only the arm, or only the leg.
- **Harder:** Hold each rep for 5 seconds.

### 6. Reverse lunge with support
Stand next to a chair or wall and hold it lightly. Step one foot back and bend both knees until the back knee is a few inches off the floor. Push through the front foot to return.
- **Easier:** Take a shorter step and lower only a little.
- **Harder:** Let go of the support.

### 7. Forearm plank
Rest on your forearms and knees or toes, elbows under your shoulders. Brace your stomach like you're about to be poked and hold.
- **Easier:** Do it on your knees, or with forearms on the seat of a chair.
- **Harder:** Hold on your toes for longer.

### 8. Standing calf raise
Hold a wall or chair, rise up onto the balls of your feet, pause, then lower slowly.
- **Harder:** Do one leg at a time.

## The 20-minute beginner routine

**Warm-up (3 minutes):** 1 minute standing march, 1 minute arm circles (forward and back), 1 minute slow chair squats without sitting all the way down.

**Main circuit (about 14 minutes):** Do each move below, rest 30 to 60 seconds between moves, and go through the full circuit twice.

| Move | Reps or time |
|---|---|
| Chair squat | 10 reps |
| Wall push-up | 10 reps |
| Glute bridge | 12 reps |
| Standing march | 45 seconds |
| Bird dog | 6 per side |
| Reverse lunge with support | 6 per leg |
| Forearm plank | 20 seconds |
| Standing calf raise | 12 reps |

**Cool-down (3 minutes):** Slow walk around the room, then a gentle stretch for your thighs, hamstrings, chest, and calves, holding each for 20 to 30 seconds.

Aim to finish each set with about 2 or 3 reps left in the tank. If you couldn't do one more rep with good form, that set was too hard. If you could have done 10 more, move to the harder option next time.

## A 4-week plan

Do the routine on three non-consecutive days, like Monday, Wednesday, and Friday. On the other days, take a 10 to 20 minute walk if you can.

- **Week 1:** Two rounds of the circuit. Use the easier option for any move that feels shaky.
- **Week 2:** Two rounds. Add 2 reps to each move, or 5 seconds to the plank and march.
- **Week 3:** Three rounds if you have time, or keep two rounds and switch one or two moves to the harder option.
- **Week 4:** Three rounds. Shorten rest to 30 seconds between moves.

## Common beginner mistakes

**Going too hard on day one.** If you can barely walk down stairs for three days, you did too much. Some soreness 24 to 48 hours after a new workout is normal, and it fades as your body adapts. Start easier than you think you need to.

**Rushing reps.** Fast, sloppy reps make the move easier and raise your injury risk. Count two seconds down and one second up.

**Holding your breath.** Breathe out on the hard part (standing up, pushing away from the wall) and breathe in on the easy part.

**Skipping rest days.** Muscles get stronger during recovery. Sleep and rest days are part of the program.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can you build muscle at home without equipment?
Yes, especially as a beginner. Bodyweight moves like squats, push-ups, lunges, and bridges give your muscles enough work to get stronger for the first several months. Once a move gets easy, make it harder by slowing down, adding reps, or switching to a tougher variation.

### How many days a week should a beginner work out at home?
Three days a week of strength work with a rest day in between is a good start. On other days, light activity like walking helps you work toward the CDC's 150 minutes of moderate activity a week.

### Can a beginner workout at home with no equipment help with weight loss?
It can help as part of the picture. Exercise builds strength and burns some calories, but weight loss mostly depends on your eating habits over time. Pair regular workouts with balanced meals and enough sleep, and check with your doctor if you have a lot of weight to lose.

### How long should a beginner workout be?
Twenty to thirty minutes, including a warm-up and cool-down, is plenty for most beginners. Even 10 minutes is worth doing on busy days. Consistency over weeks matters more than the length of any single session.

### What is the best workout for beginners at home?
A full-body routine that covers legs, pushing, hips, and core, like the one above, is a solid choice. Pick moves you can do with good form, do them a few times a week, and make them a little harder each week.
