Your workout is the signal for your muscles to get stronger. Most of the repair work happens later, while you rest, and a big share of it happens while you sleep. If you are training regularly and still feel sore, flat, or stuck, sleep is one of the first things to check.

Here is how much sleep you need for muscle recovery, what happens in your body overnight, and simple habits that help you sleep better on workout days.

## How much sleep you need

For most adults, aim for **at least 7 hours a night**. That is the CDC's recommendation for adults 18 to 60, and it matches the 2015 consensus statement from the American Academy of Sleep Medicine and the Sleep Research Society.

The CDC's guidance by age:

| Age | Recommended sleep per night |
|---|---|
| 13 to 18 years | 8 to 10 hours |
| 18 to 60 years | 7 or more hours |
| 61 to 64 years | 7 to 9 hours |
| 65 and older | 7 to 8 hours |

No official body sets a separate "muscle recovery" number. If you train hard several times a week, sleeping toward the top of your range (8 hours or a bit more) is a reasonable target. In one Stanford study of college basketball players (Mah and colleagues, 2011), players who extended their sleep to about 10 hours in bed a night for several weeks ran faster sprints and shot more accurately. That was a small group of young athletes, so treat it as a hint that extra sleep may help people who train hard.

Many people fall short. CDC survey data show roughly a third of U.S. adults report usually getting less than 7 hours.

## Why sleep matters for muscle recovery

### Growth hormone is released in deep sleep
Your body releases much of its daily growth hormone during deep sleep (also called slow-wave sleep), mostly in the first half of the night. Growth hormone helps with tissue repair and how your body uses fat and protein. Cutting your night short or sleeping in broken chunks can mean less time in deep sleep.

### Muscle protein building slows when you are short on sleep
Muscles get stronger by building new protein to repair the small amounts of damage from training. In a 2021 study in *Physiological Reports* (Lamon and colleagues), one night without sleep lowered muscle protein synthesis in young adults by about 18% compared with a normal night, and shifted hormones in a direction that favors breakdown (higher cortisol, lower testosterone). One sleepless night is extreme, but it shows how closely sleep and muscle repair are linked.

### You train better when rested
Poor sleep makes workouts feel harder, slows reaction time, and makes it easier to get sloppy with form. Over weeks, that means fewer good-quality sessions, which matters as much as the recovery itself.

### Appetite and energy
Short sleep tends to increase hunger and cravings for sugary, high-calorie food, which can work against weight loss goals and steady energy.

## Signs you are not recovering well

- Soreness that lasts more than 3 or 4 days after normal workouts
- Feeling heavy, weak, or unmotivated at the start of most sessions
- Your weights or reps going down instead of up over a few weeks
- Trouble falling asleep or waking often, especially after evening workouts
- Getting sick more often or feeling run down

If several of these sound familiar, look at your sleep before adding more exercise. Persistent fatigue, loud snoring, or gasping during sleep is worth discussing with your doctor, since it can be a sign of a sleep disorder like sleep apnea.

## 10 habits for better sleep and recovery

1. **Keep the same wake-up time every day,** including weekends. A steady wake time is the strongest anchor for your body clock.
2. **Count back from your wake time.** If you get up at 6:00 a.m. and want 7.5 hours, lights out by 10:30 p.m.
3. **Finish hard workouts 2 to 3 hours before bed** if evening exercise keeps you wired. Gentle stretching or a walk in the evening is fine for most people.
4. **Cut off caffeine by early afternoon.** Caffeine can stay in your system for many hours.
5. **Go easy on alcohol at night.** It can make you drowsy but tends to break up sleep later in the night.
6. **Keep your bedroom cool, dark, and quiet.** Blackout curtains, an eye mask, or earplugs help.
7. **Dim screens 30 to 60 minutes before bed.** Read, stretch, or take a warm shower instead.
8. **Eat enough protein through the day.** Sleep does the repair work, and protein supplies the building blocks. Our guide on [how much protein per day](academy:how-much-protein-per-day) gives simple targets.
9. **Get daylight in the morning.** A 10-minute walk outside after waking helps set your body clock.
10. **Take short naps if you need them.** 20 to 30 minutes in the early afternoon can help without wrecking your night.

## Plan rest days too

Sleep is one part of recovery. The other is giving each muscle group time between hard sessions. A simple approach for most people:

- Strength train each major muscle group 2 to 3 times a week, with at least one day between hard sessions for the same muscles.
- Use rest days for walking, light stretching, or easy mobility work.
- After a bad night, keep your workout but make it lighter: fewer sets, lighter weights, or a walk.

If you are over 40, recovery can take a little longer, so an extra rest day between hard sessions often pays off. [Strength training over 40](academy:strength-training-over-40) covers how to plan your week.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is 7 hours of sleep enough for muscle recovery?
For most adults, 7 hours meets the CDC's minimum and is enough for normal recovery from regular workouts. If you train hard most days, aim for 8 hours or a bit more, and watch how you feel and perform.

### Is 6 hours of sleep enough for muscle recovery?
Six hours is below the CDC's recommendation of at least 7 hours for adults. Some people feel fine on it for a night or two, but regularly sleeping 6 hours or less can slow recovery, make workouts feel harder, and affect overall health. Try moving bedtime 15 minutes earlier each week.

### Does sleeping help muscle recovery?
Yes. Much of your growth hormone is released during deep sleep, and research shows muscle protein synthesis drops when people are sleep deprived. Good sleep, enough protein, and rest days between hard sessions work together.

### How much deep sleep do you need for muscle recovery?
There is no official deep-sleep target. Deep sleep is a portion of each night and happens mostly in the first few hours, so getting a full 7 or more hours on a steady schedule is the most reliable way to get enough. Sleep trackers estimate deep sleep, but their numbers are not exact.

### Do naps help muscle recovery?
A short nap of 20 to 30 minutes can help you feel more alert and make up for a short night. Naps do not fully replace a good night's sleep, and long or late naps can make it harder to fall asleep at bedtime.
