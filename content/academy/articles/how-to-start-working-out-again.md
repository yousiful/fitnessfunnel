Maybe it was a new job, a baby, an injury, an illness, or a few busy years that turned into five. However long you've been away, getting back into exercise works the same way. You start lower than you think you should, you build slowly, and you keep showing up. This guide gives you a 4-week restart plan, tips to keep soreness manageable, and answers to the questions people ask most.

## First, check in with your body

Before you start, it's smart to talk with your doctor if any of these apply:

- You've been inactive for a long time and are over 50 or 60
- You have heart, lung, blood pressure, or blood sugar concerns
- You're coming back from surgery, an injury, or a serious illness
- You're pregnant or postpartum
- You have joint pain that flares with activity

A quick conversation can tell you which movements to favor and which to skip for now. During any workout, stop if you feel chest pain, dizziness, unusual shortness of breath, or sharp joint pain.

## Expect to be a beginner again (for a little while)

After a long break, your heart, lungs, and muscles lose some of their conditioning. How much depends on how long you were off, how fit you were before, and your age. The good news is that people who have trained before often regain fitness faster than they built it the first time.

The biggest mistake people make is picking up where they left off. If you used to run 5 miles or lift heavy, your mind remembers that version of you. Your tendons and muscles need time to catch up. Your first workouts should feel manageable, even a little easy.

## The 4-week restart plan

The goal of the first month is to build the habit and let your body adapt. Do strength work two or three days a week and walk on most other days. Take at least one full rest day.

### Week 1: Show up and move
- **Walk:** 10 to 15 minutes at a comfortable pace, 3 to 4 days.
- **Strength:** 2 sessions of 10 to 15 minutes. One round of chair squats (8 reps), wall push-ups (8 reps), glute bridges (10 reps), and a 15-second plank on your knees.
- **Effort:** You should be able to talk in full sentences the whole time.

### Week 2: Add a little
- **Walk:** 15 to 20 minutes, 3 to 4 days.
- **Strength:** 2 to 3 sessions. Two rounds of the same moves, adding 2 reps to each.

### Week 3: Build consistency
- **Walk:** 20 to 25 minutes. Try 1 minute of brisker walking every 4 or 5 minutes.
- **Strength:** 3 sessions of 20 minutes. Add bird dogs (6 per side) and supported reverse lunges (6 per leg).

### Week 4: Settle into a routine
- **Walk:** 25 to 30 minutes, 4 days.
- **Strength:** 3 sessions. Two to three rounds, rest 30 to 60 seconds between moves.

By the end of week 4 you're close to the CDC recommendation of 150 minutes of moderate activity a week plus 2 days of muscle strengthening. A simple rule for progressing: change only one thing per week. Add a few minutes, or a few reps, or one round, and leave everything else the same.

## How to start working out again without getting sore

Some soreness 1 to 2 days after a new workout is normal. It's called delayed onset muscle soreness, and it tends to be worst after the first few sessions. These habits keep it manageable:

- **Do less than you can.** End each set with 2 or 3 reps left in the tank.
- **Warm up for 3 to 5 minutes.** March in place, circle your arms, and do a few slow squats.
- **Go slow on the lowering part of each move.** It builds control, but too much of it too soon is what makes you sore, so keep total reps low in week 1.
- **Keep moving on sore days.** An easy walk usually feels better than sitting still.
- **Sleep and eat enough.** Your body rebuilds during rest, and it needs protein and overall food to do that.

If soreness is so bad you can't sit down or climb stairs normally, or it lasts more than a few days, scale back your next session by half.

## Returning after an injury or illness

If you're coming back from an injury, follow the guidance of your doctor or physical therapist first. They'll often clear you for some movements before others. Work around the injured area, not through it.

After being sick, wait until fever and major symptoms are gone. Start with short, easy walks and give it a few extra days before strength work. If you feel wiped out after a short session, take another rest day.

## Coming back at 50 or later

You can absolutely start again in your 50s, 60s, and beyond. Strength training becomes more valuable with age because it helps you keep muscle, balance, and the ability to do everyday things like carrying groceries and getting up from the floor. Start with supported moves (a chair, a wall, a counter), add balance practice like standing on one foot while holding the counter, and give yourself an extra recovery day if you need it. ## Make it stick this time

Motivation fades for everyone. What keeps people going is a routine that's easy to repeat.

- **Pick your days and times now.** "Monday, Wednesday, Friday at 7 a.m." is easier to follow than "three times a week."
- **Keep it short.** A 15-minute workout you do beats a 60-minute workout you skip.
- **Track it.** Writing down each session shows you progress you'd otherwise miss. Our [free workout tracker](/app/) logs your workouts and keeps a streak so you can see the weeks add up.
- **Plan for missed days.** You will miss some. Pick up with the next scheduled session instead of waiting for Monday.
- **Find your people.** A friend, a coach, or a group who expects to see you makes a big difference.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long does it take to get back in shape after a long break?
It depends on how long you were off, your age, and your fitness before the break. Many people notice workouts feel easier within 2 to 4 weeks of steady training, and bigger changes in strength and stamina show up over 2 to 3 months. Consistency matters more than how hard any one session is.

### What should my first workout back be?
Something short and easy, like a 10 to 15 minute walk plus one round of squats, wall push-ups, and glute bridges. You should finish feeling like you could have done more. That leaves you ready to do it again in two days.

### How do I start working out again after years of not exercising?
Start with walking and simple bodyweight moves two or three days a week, and increase only one thing each week. If you're over 50 or have health conditions, check with your doctor first. Expect some soreness early on, and keep your effort at a level where you can still talk.

### Is it normal to be sore after working out again?
Yes. Soreness that shows up 1 to 2 days after a workout is common when you're starting back. It usually fades as your body adapts over the first couple of weeks. Sharp pain, joint pain, or soreness that doesn't ease after a few days is worth getting checked.

### Should I start working out again at home or at the gym?
Either works. Home workouts remove the commute and the intimidation factor, which helps many people stay consistent in the first months. Pick the option you're most likely to keep doing.
